# The Bed
Four square metres, dug in the second week of March, in ground that had been lawn for
longer than anyone could remember. The turf came off in strips and went upside down at
the bottom of the trench, which is the oldest trick in the subject and still the best
one: the grass rots where it cannot reach the light, and feeds what is planted above it.

The dimensions matter more than they sound. **A bed two metres deep is a bed with a
middle you cannot reach**, and a middle you cannot reach is where the weeds win. Two
metres by two, with a path on all four sides, means every square of it is an arm's
length from somewhere you can stand.

> A bed you can reach across is a bed you will actually weed.

## Aspect
It faces south and west, which gives it the afternoon. The wall behind holds heat until
about ten at night in high summer and keeps the frost off a fortnight longer than the
open ground at the far end. Nothing in these notes would transfer unaltered to a bed
that faces north.

# What Went In
In order, and with the dates, because the order turned out to matter more than the
choices.

**Broad beans**, sown direct on the eighteenth of March, two rows, twenty centimetres
apart. They are the only thing here that will germinate in cold, wet soil without
sulking, and they were up in eleven days.

**Onion sets**, the last week of March, pushed in until just the tips showed. Birds
pulled up nine of them in the first three days, which is normal and is why you push them
in rather than bury them: they are easy to put back.

**Potatoes**, first earlies only, mid-April, in a trench a spade deep. They take a
quarter of the bed for three months and they are the reason the rest of the plan had to
be drawn on paper first.

---

**Lettuce, radish and rocket**, from late April, sown in short rows a fortnight apart
rather than all at once. The single most useful habit in the whole season. A metre of
radish sown on one day gives you more radish than any household can eat in the four days
they are worth eating, and then none at all.

**Runner beans**, the last week of May, once the frosts had genuinely finished, up a
frame of four canes tied at the top.

# What It Taught
Three things, and only the third was a surprise.

**Successional sowing is the whole game.** Nearly every failure of abundance here was a
failure of timing rather than of growing — too much at once, then a gap, then too much
again.

**The soil is the crop.** What went into the trench in March did more for August than
anything applied to the surface later.

**And the bed was too small, which is the right mistake.** A bed that is slightly too
small stays weeded, stays watered, and stays interesting for a whole season. A bed that
is slightly too large becomes a chore in June and a wilderness by August, and teaches
nothing at all except that you should have dug a smaller one.

> Dig the bed you will still be visiting in August.
